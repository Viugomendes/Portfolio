## An unexpected debt

In the *Phaedo*, Plato tells us that, just before dying, Socrates asked Crito: "We owe a rooster to Asclepius. Don't forget to pay the debt." It is not a grand farewell, nor a line designed to look good in history. It is a simple, almost domestic reminder, addressed to someone he trusted. And precisely for that reason it keeps turning in your mind: why think about a debt when life is about to end?

Asclepius was the Greek god of medicine. A traditional explanation reads the rooster as an offering of thanks for a cure. But the scene does not stop to explain it, and that missing answer opens a larger question: what did Socrates think of the life he was leaving behind? For him, to philosophize had meant examining his own ideas, acknowledging what he did not know, and trying to act justly. Even at the end, he seems to care about keeping a pending promise.

## Nietzsche's reading

Centuries later, Nietzsche returns to those words in *Twilight of the Idols*. His reading is uncomfortable: he hears in the debt to the god of medicine a sign that Socrates considered life a sickness and death a kind of cure. We don't have to accept that interpretation as the definitive explanation. What is interesting is that Nietzsche forces us to ask whether Socrates takes his leave grateful for life or, on the contrary, tired of it.

The question matters because living does not always feel easy. There are days of uncertainty, mistakes, and conversations we would rather avoid. Nietzsche distrusts answers that turn suffering into a reason to despise existence. His invitation, understood carefully, is not to pretend everything is fine, but to ask ourselves what we do with what we are given to live. Life does not need to be perfect to deserve our attention.

## Choosing the concrete life

For me, Socrates' small debt can also be read as a form of responsibility. We don't know for certain what the rooster meant, but we do see someone who, right to the end, remembers that his actions affect other people. There is a difference between talking about values and keeping a small promise. Trust is usually built in that space: really listening, admitting a mistake, asking for help in time, and leaving things a little better than we found them.

That too is a way of living. It is not only the great projects or the exceptional moments that count; what counts is the curiosity with which we learn, the respect we show in a disagreement, and the attention we pay to those working beside us. On a team, those everyday decisions are what let people think freely, make mistakes without hiding them, and try again.

Maybe Socrates owed a rooster for a cure. Maybe Nietzsche saw in the line a weary farewell. We don't have to fully resolve that ambiguity to take away something useful: life is fragile and, for that very reason, deserves to be lived consciously. For me, living it well does not mean always having an answer; it means going on asking questions, taking responsibility for my decisions, and not forgetting the people I share the road with.
