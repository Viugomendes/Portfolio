## Nobody puts friendship on a CV

Skills, certifications, years of experience. That is what fits on a page. But when I think about the teams I would want to join, the thing I care about most is not on any CV: whether the people in the room actually trust each other.

Philosophers spent a couple of thousand years on exactly this question. They just never called it teamwork.

## Who is at the table

Epicurus put friendship at the top of everything wisdom can give you for a happy life. Seneca quotes him with a piece of advice I find oddly practical: before you think about what you are going to eat, think about who you are eating with. The company matters more than the meal. Swap "meal" for "project" and it still works.

## Three kinds, only one that lasts

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle splits friendship into three: friendships of utility, of pleasure, and of virtue. The first two are perfectly real and perfectly fragile. They last as long as the usefulness or the fun does. Change jobs and they fade, no hard feelings.

The third kind is different. You value the other person for who they are, and you want them to do well for their own sake, not for what it gets you. Aristotle says these are rare and take time, which sounds right to me. A lot of professional networking is the first kind, and that is fine. But the colleagues people still talk about ten years later usually belong to the third. Nobody builds that in a kickoff meeting.

## Your best enemy

Yes, Nietzsche again. In *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* he says that in a friend you should have your best enemy. I read that less as a taste for conflict and more as a standard: a friend who agrees with everything is just an echo. You want the person who will tell you, kindly, that your idea has a hole in it.

Seneca asks for the same thing from the other side. Take your time deciding whom to trust, he says, but once you have decided, trust them fully. No half measures.

In security work this is not abstract. A report only gets better if somebody reads it and says "I don't think this finding holds up", ideally before the client does. That only happens where people feel safe enough to say it, and safe enough to hear it.

## What I try to bring

I am not claiming to have this figured out. What I try to do is simple: ask for help before I am stuck rather than after, say what I really think in a way the other person can use, give credit where it is due, and be around when something breaks at an inconvenient hour. None of it is glamorous. All of it is what a good team feels like from the inside.
